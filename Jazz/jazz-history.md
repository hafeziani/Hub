# A Schematic History of Jazz

A era-by-era map of how jazz evolved, who drove each shift, and the albums worth hearing to understand it. Jazz didn't move in a straight line — styles overlapped, coexisted, and looped back on each other — but this rough chronology is the standard way historians and musicians frame it.

## Roots (1890s–1900s): Blues, Ragtime, and New Orleans

Jazz emerged in New Orleans around the turn of the 20th century, at the meeting point of African rhythmic traditions, blues, ragtime piano, brass-band marches, and Creole and Caribbean influences. Congo Square, where enslaved and free Black New Orleanians gathered to play music on Sundays, is often cited as a symbolic origin point. No jazz recordings exist from this period — the music was still oral and local — but ragtime (Scott Joplin) and the blues (W.C. Handy) are its direct ancestors and worth hearing to understand what jazz grew out of.

**Listen to:** Scott Joplin, *piano rolls and rags* (recorded posthumously by others, since Joplin died in 1917 before jazz was captured on record).

## New Orleans and Dixieland Jazz (1900s–1920s)

The first jazz recordings appeared in 1917 (the Original Dixieland Jass Band), but the more historically important figures are Buddy Bolden (unrecorded, but considered the first jazz musician by reputation), Jelly Roll Morton, King Oliver, and above all Louis Armstrong, whose Hot Five and Hot Seven sessions redefined jazz around the soloist rather than collective ensemble playing.

**Must-listen albums:**
- Louis Armstrong, *The Hot Fives and Hot Sevens* (recorded 1925–1928) — the moment jazz becomes about the individual improviser.
- Jelly Roll Morton, *The Complete Library of Congress Recordings* or his Red Hot Peppers sides (1926–1927) — New Orleans ensemble jazz at its most sophisticated.
- King Oliver's Creole Jazz Band, *Off the Record: The Complete 1923 Jazz Band Recordings*.

## Swing and the Big Band Era (1930s–1940s)

Jazz became America's popular dance music. Big bands, tight arrangements, and star soloists defined the sound, and it's the era when jazz was mainstream entertainment rather than a subculture.

**Key figures:** Duke Ellington, Count Basie, Benny Goodman, Fletcher Henderson, Chick Webb, Billie Holiday, Ella Fitzgerald.

**Must-listen albums:**
- Duke Ellington, *The Blanton-Webster Band* (1940–1942) — widely considered his greatest working band.
- Count Basie, *The Complete Decca Recordings* (1937–1939) — the definitive swing rhythm section.
- Billie Holiday, *Lady Day: The Complete Billie Holiday on Columbia* (1933–1944).
- Benny Goodman, *Live at Carnegie Hall* (1938) — the concert that legitimized jazz as concert music.

## Bebop (mid-1940s)

A deliberate break from swing: fast tempos, complex harmony, small combos instead of big bands, and improvisation as the whole point rather than a garnish. Born in after-hours jam sessions in Harlem (notably at Minton's Playhouse).

**Key figures:** Charlie Parker, Dizzy Gillespie, Thelonious Monk, Bud Powell, Max Roach.

**Must-listen albums:**
- Charlie Parker, *Charlie Parker with Strings* or *The Complete Savoy and Dial Studio Recordings* — Parker's small-group Dial/Savoy sides (1945–1948) are the bebop cornerstone.
- Dizzy Gillespie, *Groovin' High* (1945).
- Thelonious Monk, *Genius of Modern Music, Vol. 1* (1947–1948).

## Cool Jazz and West Coast Jazz (late 1940s–1950s)

A reaction to bebop's intensity: lighter tone, more arrangement, slower tempos, influences from classical music. Centered partly on the West Coast but starting with Miles Davis in New York.

**Key figures:** Miles Davis, Gerry Mulligan, Chet Baker, Dave Brubeck, Lee Konitz.

**Must-listen albums:**
- Miles Davis, *Birth of the Cool* (recorded 1949–1950, released 1957) — the record that named the movement.
- Dave Brubeck Quartet, *Time Out* (1959) — unusual time signatures, the first jazz album to sell a million copies.
- Chet Baker, *Chet Baker Sings* (1954).

## Hard Bop (1950s)

Bebop's harder-edged, more blues- and gospel-inflected successor, developed largely on the East Coast as a response to cool jazz's restraint.

**Key figures:** Art Blakey, Horace Silver, Sonny Rollins, Clifford Brown, Max Roach.

**Must-listen albums:**
- Art Blakey and the Jazz Messengers, *Moanin'* (1958).
- Sonny Rollins, *Saxophone Colossus* (1956).
- Clifford Brown and Max Roach, *Clifford Brown and Max Roach* (1954).
- Horace Silver, *Song for My Father* (1964, though rooted in the hard bop language of the prior decade).

## Modal Jazz (late 1950s–1960s)

Instead of improvising over dense chord changes, musicians improvised over static scales/modes, opening up more melodic freedom. This era produced the best-selling and most widely recommended jazz album ever made.

**Key figures:** Miles Davis, John Coltrane, Bill Evans.

**Must-listen albums:**
- Miles Davis, *Kind of Blue* (1959) — if someone listens to only one jazz album ever, it's this one.
- John Coltrane, *A Love Supreme* (1965) — a four-part spiritual suite, arguably the second-most-essential jazz album.
- Bill Evans Trio, *Sunday at the Village Vanguard* (1961).

## Free Jazz and the Avant-Garde (1960s)

A rejection of fixed chords, tempo, and even conventional tonality, running parallel to the era's social upheaval. Divisive at the time, hugely influential since.

**Key figures:** Ornette Coleman, Cecil Taylor, Albert Ayler, Sun Ra, late-period Coltrane.

**Must-listen albums:**
- Ornette Coleman, *The Shape of Jazz to Come* (1959) and *Free Jazz* (1961, the album that gave the movement its name).
- John Coltrane, *Ascension* (1965).
- Cecil Taylor, *Unit Structures* (1966).

## Post-Bop (1960s)

Musicians who kept swing and harmonic sophistication but absorbed modal and free innovations without going fully outside — often described as the most harmonically advanced era of "straight-ahead" jazz.

**Key figures:** Wayne Shorter, Herbie Hancock, the Miles Davis Second Great Quintet (with Shorter, Hancock, Ron Carter, Tony Williams).

**Must-listen albums:**
- Herbie Hancock, *Maiden Voyage* (1965).
- Wayne Shorter, *Speak No Evil* (1966).
- Miles Davis Quintet, *Nefertiti* (1968).

## Jazz Fusion (late 1960s–1970s)

Jazz merges with rock, funk, and electric instrumentation. Divisive among purists, foundational for everything that follows in jazz-adjacent popular music.

**Key figures:** Miles Davis, Weather Report, Herbie Hancock, Chick Corea and Return to Forever, Mahavishnu Orchestra.

**Must-listen albums:**
- Miles Davis, *Bitches Brew* (1970) — the record that detonated the genre.
- Weather Report, *Heavy Weather* (1977), featuring "Birdland."
- Herbie Hancock, *Head Hunters* (1973) — jazz-funk, hugely commercially successful.
- Mahavishnu Orchestra, *The Inner Mounting Flame* (1971).

## Smooth Jazz and Jazz-Funk (1970s–1980s)

A more commercial, radio-friendly branch of fusion, often criticized by jazz purists as watered down but genuinely popular and technically accomplished.

**Key figures:** Grover Washington Jr., George Benson, Bob James.

**Must-listen albums:**
- George Benson, *Breezin'* (1976).
- Grover Washington Jr., *Winelight* (1980).

## Neo-Traditionalism / The Young Lions (1980s–1990s)

A conscious return to acoustic, pre-fusion jazz values, spearheaded by Wynton Marsalis and institutionalized through Jazz at Lincoln Center. Simultaneously, downtown NYC musicians (John Zorn, the "Knitting Factory" scene) kept the avant-garde alive.

**Key figures:** Wynton Marsalis, Branford Marsalis, John Zorn.

**Must-listen albums:**
- Wynton Marsalis, *Black Codes (From the Underground)* (1985).
- John Zorn, *Naked City* (1989).

## Contemporary Jazz (2000s–present)

No single dominant style — jazz today draws on hip-hop, neo-soul, electronic music, odd-meter grooves, and the entire preceding canon at once, often by musicians who move fluidly between jazz and other genres.

**Key figures:** Kamasi Washington, Robert Glasper, Esperanza Spalding, Brad Mehldau, Christian Scott aTunde Adjuah, Vijay Iyer.

**Must-listen albums:**
- Kamasi Washington, *The Epic* (2015).
- Robert Glasper Experiment, *Black Radio* (2012).
- Esperanza Spalding, *Emily's D+Evolution* (2016).
- Brad Mehldau, *Art of the Trio, Vol. 2: Live at the Village Vanguard* (1998) or *Highway Rider* (2010).

## A 15-Album Starter Path (if you're building a jazz collection from scratch)

Listened to roughly in this order, this gives a working sense of the whole arc:

1. Louis Armstrong, *The Hot Fives and Hot Sevens*
2. Duke Ellington, *The Blanton-Webster Band*
3. Billie Holiday, *Lady Day: The Complete Billie Holiday on Columbia*
4. Charlie Parker, *The Complete Savoy and Dial Studio Recordings*
5. Thelonious Monk, *Genius of Modern Music, Vol. 1*
6. Miles Davis, *Birth of the Cool*
7. Dave Brubeck Quartet, *Time Out*
8. Sonny Rollins, *Saxophone Colossus*
9. Art Blakey and the Jazz Messengers, *Moanin'*
10. Miles Davis, *Kind of Blue*
11. John Coltrane, *A Love Supreme*
12. Ornette Coleman, *The Shape of Jazz to Come*
13. Herbie Hancock, *Maiden Voyage*
14. Miles Davis, *Bitches Brew*
15. Kamasi Washington, *The Epic*

A note on sourcing: this overview reflects widely agreed-upon jazz-historical consensus (the kind found in standard references like Ted Gioia's *The History of Jazz* or the Ken Burns *Jazz* documentary), not a single disputed reading — but as with any genre history, specialists will quibble over emphasis and boundaries between eras.
